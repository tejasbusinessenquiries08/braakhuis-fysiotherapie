What Causes Lower Back Pain in Females?

M
Mark Braakhuis
HCPC Registered Physiotherapist, MCSP
Direct Answer

Lower back pain in females usually comes down to one of two things: a mechanical joint or muscle issue, or internal pain linked to your monthly cycle or pelvic organs. If your back hurts more when you bend, lift, or sit, it is almost certainly a joint or muscle problem. If your ache flares up around your period, feels deep inside your pelvis, or stays exactly the same no matter how you sit or stand, it may be linked to your hormones or reproductive system.

The short answer: movement pain versus cycle pain

Key RulePain that changes when you move or bend is usually a joint or muscle problem. Pain that stays identical regardless of position is often internal.

Most lower back pain is mechanical. That means a joint, disc, or muscle in your spine is sensitive to movement or posture. This type of pain changes when you shift position: standing up might ease it, or sitting on a soft sofa might make it ache.

In women, however, the lower spine sits right next to the uterus, ovaries, and pelvic floor. Nerve pathways from these organs share space in the lower spinal cord. When an organ in your pelvis is inflamed or cramping, your brain often feels that ache in your lower back.

Working out whether your ache changes with physical movement is the first step in finding the right help.

Joint and muscle causes common in women

Women have a wider pelvis than men. This changes the angle of the hips and puts extra load on the sacroiliac joint, which is the joint connecting your lower spine to your pelvic bone. When this joint moves unevenly or becomes irritated, it causes a sharp ache on one side of your lower back or buttock.

Pregnancy and childbirth also change how the core and pelvic floor muscles work together. During pregnancy, hormones loosen the ligaments in your pelvis to prepare for birth. If the deep stabilizing muscles do not regain their full strength afterwards, the joints in your lower back carry more load during everyday tasks like lifting children or carrying shopping.

Prolonged sitting at a desk can also cause simple postural fatigue. Sitting in one position for hours does not slip a disc or damage your bones, but it keeps your lower back in a rounded position. This fluid shift leaves the tissues feeling stiff and sore when you first stand up.

  • •Sacroiliac joint irritation: ache on one side of the lower back, often worse when standing on one leg or climbing stairs.
  • •Pelvic floor and deep core weakness: loss of coordination after pregnancy, leaving the lower back to work overtime.
  • •Postural stiffness from desk work: a dull ache across the beltline that fades once you walk around for ten minutes.

Hormonal and pelvic causes of lower back ache

Your monthly menstrual cycle can cause lower back ache. Just before your period starts, your body releases chemical messengers called prostaglandins. These make the uterus contract to shed its lining. Strong contractions can send referred aching signals straight into the lower back and thighs.

Conditions affecting the pelvic organs can also trigger back pain. Endometriosis occurs when tissue similar to the lining of the womb grows outside the uterus, such as on the ovaries or fallopian tubes. This tissue bleeds during each cycle, causing inflammation and deep lower back pain.

Adenomyosis and uterine fibroids are two other common conditions. Fibroids are non-cancerous growths in the muscle wall of the womb. If a fibroid grows large enough, it can press backward toward the spine, creating a heavy, dragging ache in the lower back.

  • •Monthly period cramps: aching across the lower back during the first 24 to 48 hours of bleeding.
  • •Endometriosis: deep pelvic pain and lower back ache that often worsens mid-cycle or during your period.
  • •Uterine fibroids: a feeling of fullness or pressure in the lower stomach accompanied by constant back tiredness.

How to tell if your back pain is spinal or internal

You do not need a medical degree to notice how your body behaves. Pay attention to how your ache reacts to daily movements.

Spinal joint and muscle pain almost always has a physical trigger. If your back hurts when you tie your shoes, lift a laundry basket, or get out of a car, you are dealing with a musculoskeletal problem. A change in position usually changes the intensity.

Pelvic or hormonal back pain rarely cares whether you are standing, sitting, or lying flat. It tends to follow the calendar rather than your movement. If your back aches constantly for two days every month, or if you also have heavy bleeding, pain during intercourse, or severe period cramps, speak to your GP.

Red flags: when to see a doctor straight away

Emergency Red FlagsDo not wait for an appointment if you notice any of these warning signs.

The vast majority of lower back pain is not dangerous. However, there are specific warning signs that require emergency medical attention rather than a routine clinic visit.

If you experience any of the symptoms listed below, go straight to your nearest hospital emergency department (A&E) or call emergency services.

  • •Loss of control of your bladder or bowel, or losing the sensation when you wipe after using the toilet.
  • •Numbness or pins and needles between your legs, around your genitals, or around your back passage (saddle anaesthesia).
  • •Sudden, progressive weakness in both legs, such as your feet catching or dragging when you try to walk.
  • •Severe lower back pain accompanied by high fever, chills, or sudden unexplained weight loss.
  • •Back pain that began immediately after a high-impact fall, road traffic collision, or major physical trauma.

When NOT to book physiotherapy

Physiotherapy is an active treatment for joints, tendons, and muscles. It is not the right choice for every type of pain.

Do not book a physiotherapy appointment if you have unexplained pelvic bleeding, a suspected broken bone, or a burning sensation when you urinate accompanied by fever. These issues need assessment by your GP, a gynaecologist, or an urgent treatment centre.

We also tell people not to book with us if they only want passive massage without doing any home exercises. In our records of 18,420 completed treatments, 82% of patients finish care within 4 to 6 sessions because they practice simple daily movements at home. Hands-on table treatment alone does not rebuild long-term strength.

What a 45-minute physiotherapy assessment checks

If your lower back pain is mechanical, an initial appointment takes 45 minutes and costs £75. You sit down with a registered physiotherapist who listens to your history and tests how your joints move.

We check your spinal flexibility, test whether bending forward or backward relieves your discomfort, and assess the strength of your hips and core muscles. We also examine your sacroiliac joints to see if one side is moving stiffly.

Before you leave, you receive a plain explanation of what is causing your ache and three specific exercises to start at home. We do not sell 15-session blocks up front. If active movement does not begin easing your pain within three sessions, we review your plan or refer you for medical imaging.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can hormonal changes during my period cause lower back pain?

Yes. Hormones called prostaglandins cause your womb to contract during your period. These muscular contractions can send referred aching pain through the shared nerves in your pelvis into your lower back and thighs.

How do I know if my lower back pain is coming from my ovaries or my spine?

Spinal pain changes with posture and movement. It usually feels sharper when you bend, twist, or lift, and eases when you find a comfortable resting position. Pain from the ovaries or uterus tends to stay constant regardless of posture and often follows your monthly cycle.

Why does sacroiliac (SI) joint pain affect women more frequently than men?

Women have a wider pelvis and slightly different joint angles to allow for childbearing. This places higher twisting forces on the sacroiliac joints. Hormonal shifts during pregnancy also soften the ligaments supporting these joints.

Can weak pelvic floor muscles trigger persistent lower back stiffness?

Yes. The pelvic floor works as the bottom anchor of your core muscle system. When pelvic floor muscles are weakened or uncoordinated, the muscles and joints of the lower back have to brace harder to support your spine.

When should I see a GP instead of a physiotherapist for lower back pain?

See your GP if your back pain is accompanied by unusual vaginal discharge, severe pelvic cramping, burning during urination, fever, or pain that does not change at all with movement.
